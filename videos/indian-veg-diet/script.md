**Voiceover:**

Try to include three things in each main meal.

First, a protein source—such as dal, chickpeas, kidney beans, peas, soy chunks, tofu, paneer, milk, or curd.

Second, vegetables or fruit.

Third, staple foods such as roti, rice, poha, or oats. Adjust the portion to suit your goal and appetite.

ICMR-NIN recognizes pulses as an important protein source in vegetarian Indian diets. Combinations such as dal with roti or dal with rice can work well in everyday meals.

If you're a student with long study sessions, here's one simple example of a day.

For breakfast, you could have besan chilla with curd, or oats with milk.

Here's an example portion: two chillas made with 60 grams of besan, vegetables, 150 grams of curd, and one teaspoon of oil—roughly 20 to 22 grams of protein, 40 to 45 grams of carbohydrates, and 12 to 15 grams of fat. The oats option—40 grams of oats with 250 millilitres of toned milk—provides roughly 13 grams of protein, 40 grams of carbohydrates, and 9 to 11 grams of fat.

For lunch, have roti or rice, dal or chickpeas, and vegetables.

A sample meal of two rotis made with a total of 60 grams of flour, one 200-millilitre bowl of cooked dal or chickpeas, 150 grams of vegetables, and one teaspoon of oil may provide roughly 18 to 24 grams of protein, 70 to 95 grams of carbohydrates, and 9 to 15 grams of fat.

For a study snack, have fruit with roasted chana. One medium piece of fruit with 30 grams of roasted chana may provide roughly 7 to 8 grams of protein, 40 to 45 grams of carbohydrates, and 2 to 4 grams of fat.

For dinner, have roti or rice with soy, paneer, chickpeas, kidney beans, or dal, along with vegetables.

Here's a soy-based example: two rotis made with 60 grams of flour, 30 grams of dry soy chunks, 150 grams of vegetables, and one teaspoon of oil. This may provide roughly 24 to 26 grams of protein, 60 to 70 grams of carbohydrates, and 8 to 12 grams of fat. The numbers will differ if you use paneer, dal, or kidney beans. Paneer type and cooking oil can especially change the fat content.

These are recipe estimates, not exact lab values. Bowl size, brand, recipe, and the amount of oil can all change the macros.

For reference, ICMR-NIN's 2,000-calorie vegetarian "My Plate" provides about 72 grams of protein and 66 grams of fat, with 55 percent of its energy coming from carbohydrates. At 2,000 calories, that works out to about 275 grams of carbohydrates. This is a reference day, not everyone's ideal target. Your daily needs depend on your body size, activity, health, and goals.

If you work, take this same meal pattern in your tiffin. Instead of choosing only roti or rice at the canteen, ask, "Do you have dal, chole, or rajma?" Keep fruit or roasted chana in your bag for a snack.

If you manage a household, use the family's dal and vegetables as your base. Keep curd or roasted chana ready for a quick snack. You don't need to make a separate fitness meal.

If you're on a tight budget, dal, chickpeas, roasted chana, and soy chunks can be practical options. If you're vegan, choose tofu or other soy-based foods.

**Voiceover:**

In a randomized crossover experiment from 2012, 19 adults with overweight tried different conditions: sitting continuously, or taking two minutes of light or moderate walking after every 20 minutes of sitting.

The walking-break conditions had lower post-meal glucose and insulin responses. This was a small, short-term lab study. It does not prove that taking study breaks directly causes fat loss. But short walks between long periods of sitting can be a practical way to add movement.

You don't have to turn every study or work break into a workout. Walk a little, refill your water, and then get back to work.

Now, let's do a rough walking calculation. First, a correction: 1,500 steps in 30 minutes is not everyone's average—that works out to 50 steps per minute. For adults, 100 steps per minute is often used as a rough research-based cue for moderate-intensity walking. At that pace, 30 minutes would be about 3,000 steps.

For illustration only, if we use a rough estimate of 3 to 6 calories per 100 steps: 1,500 divided by 100, multiplied by 3 to 6, gives about 45 to 90 calories. At 4 to 5 calories per 100 steps, that's about 60 to 75 calories.

If you take three 30-minute walks in a day—around breakfast, lunch, and your evening snack—that adds up to 90 minutes and 4,500 steps. Using the same rough formula, that's about 135 to 270 calories. Four walks—around breakfast, lunch, your evening snack, and dinner—add up to two hours and 6,000 steps, or roughly 180 to 360 calories.

This per-step formula is not a precise or universal way to measure calories. Actual burn varies with body weight, walking speed, stride, and route. For comparison, the CDC estimates about 140 calories per 30 minutes for a 70-kilo adult walking at 3.5 miles per hour; a lighter or slower walk may burn less. Don't treat these numbers as a guaranteed calorie deficit or fat loss. Spread your walks through the day in a way that fits your routine and feels comfortable.

Now let's talk about fat loss. Over time, fat loss requires taking in slightly less energy from food and drinks than your body uses. That doesn't mean you have to stop eating roti or rice. Start by looking at easy-to-miss items such as sugary drinks, biscuits, frequent fried snacks, and extra oil.

Just to explain the maths: if an adult's estimated maintenance intake is 2,200 calories, reducing that by 300 gives 1,900 calories. That is not a personal target for anyone. Actual calorie needs differ from person to person, and not everyone needs to count calories. If tracking your weight works for you, look at the overall trend over several weeks rather than focusing on one day's number.

**Voiceover:**

Building muscle requires regular resistance training, enough food, and recovery. The WHO recommends that adults get at least 150 minutes of moderate activity each week, along with muscle-strengthening activity on two or more days. If you're currently inactive, start at a manageable level and build up gradually.

Now let's calculate protein. Research suggests that around 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram per day may be a rough range for healthy adults doing resistance training. The exact amount is not the same for everyone.

Here's an example for a 60-kilo adult: 60 times 1.2 equals 72 grams per day. 60 times 1.6 equals 96 grams per day.

So the rough example range is 72 to 96 grams per day. If you divide that across four meals, that comes to about 18 to 24 grams per meal. This is only an example, not a personal prescription. If you have a medical condition, discuss your target with a clinician or dietitian.

Use dal, chickpeas, kidney beans, soy chunks, tofu, paneer, milk, and curd in vegetarian meals. Check the nutrition label on packaged foods—the protein amount varies by brand and serving size.

Here's a research finding: a meta-analysis combining 49 studies and 1,863 adults found that adding protein alongside resistance training led to a small average improvement in results. The pooled estimate showed about 0.3 kilos of additional fat-free mass. Beyond roughly 1.6 grams of protein per kilo per day, muscle-mass gains did not appear to increase further, though the estimate had some uncertainty.

These studies don't directly represent every Indian vegetarian. Treat this as a general research finding, not a personal prescription.

The practical point is that training is your main stimulus. Protein powder is not compulsory.

If you're hungry before a workout, you could have something simple like a banana, curd, or milk. After your workout, have a normal home-cooked meal—such as dal and rice, roti with paneer and vegetables, or soy with roti. You don't need a shake immediately after every workout.
