# Anxiety and stress-related disorders: Davidson 24th ed (2022) notes

Paraphrased from Davidson's Principles & Practice of Medicine, 24th ed. Printed page numbers.

## Anxiety disorders (p1254–1255)
- Defined by the emotion of anxiety, worrisome thoughts, avoidance behaviours and somatic symptoms of autonomic arousal (p1254).
- The somatic symptoms often make patients present to medical services first. Anxiety may be stress-related, a phobia may follow an unpleasant incident, and many patients with anxiety also have depression (p1254).
- Three subtypes (Box 31.29, p1254):

| | Phobic | Panic | Generalised (GAD) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Occurrence | Situational | Paroxysmal | Persistent |
| Behaviour | Avoidance | Escape | Agitation |
| Cognition | Fear of the situation | Fear of the symptoms | Worry |
| Symptoms | On exposure | Episodic | Persistent |

- **Phobia:** abnormal or excessive fear of a specific object or situation, leading to avoidance (e.g. fear of an air crash → avoiding flying). Agoraphobia = general phobia of going out alone or being in crowded places. Phobias can develop to medical procedures such as venepuncture (p1255).
- **Panic disorder:** repeated attacks of severe anxiety, not restricted to a situation. Somatic symptoms are common: chest pain, palpitations, paraesthesia in lips and fingers. They are partly due to involuntary over-breathing (hyperventilation). Patients often fear a heart attack or stroke and seek emergency care. Can coexist with agoraphobia (p1255).
- **GAD:** recurrent or chronic anxiety with uncontrollable worry. Muscle tension leads to various medical presentations (p1255).
- **Diagnosis:** clinical. Panic is confirmed by asking the patient to hyperventilate deliberately for 1–2 min and seeing whether the symptoms are reproduced. Respiratory alkalosis on ABG indicates chronic hyperventilation (p1255).
- **Management, psychological:** explanation and reassurance are essential (especially if the patient fears a serious illness); relaxation; graded exposure (desensitisation) for phobias; CBT (p1255).
- **Management, drugs:**
  - Antidepressants are first choice. The dose can be higher and the response slower than in depression. Escitalopram, duloxetine and clomipramine show the most efficacy in their classes. Activating side-effects can worsen anxiety in the first 2 weeks; warn the patient (p1255).
  - Benzodiazepines help short term, but regular long-term use carries a high risk of dependence. Limit regular prescriptions to 3 weeks, then occasional use only (p1255).
  - Lorazepam: rapid onset, relief up to 2 h, highest dependence potential. Diazepam: about 1 h to act orally, relief up to 12 h. Pregabalin is an alternative (also tolerance/dependence). Propranolol helps prominent somatic symptoms (p1255).
- The anxiety subtypes are more common in women. OCD is equally common in men and women (p1255).

## Anxiety in medical patients (p1242)
- Box 31.11, differential diagnosis of anxiety:
  - normal response to threat, adjustment disorder, GAD, panic disorder, phobic disorder;
  - **organic causes:** hyperthyroidism, paroxysmal arrhythmias, phaeochromocytoma, alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal, hypoglycaemia, temporal lobe epilepsy.
- Benzodiazepines are very effective short term but cause tolerance and unpleasant or dangerous withdrawal if used for more than a few weeks.
- Anxiety and depression are the most common psychiatric diagnoses in the medically ill. Anxiety may show as more somatic symptoms (breathlessness, tremor, palpitations) or as avoidance of treatment.

## OCD (p1255)
- **Obsessions:** recurrent, unwanted, anxiety-provoking thoughts, images or impulses, recognised as one's own. **Compulsions:** repeated acts to relieve the anxiety.
- **Examples:** contamination fear → ritual hand-washing; fear of having forgotten something → repeated checking.
- **Differential diagnosis:** normal checking; delusions of thought possession.
- **Management:** antidepressants (high-dose clomipramine or SSRI) and exposure response prevention (a CBT method: face the feared thought without performing the compulsion). Relapse is common; it is often chronic.

## Stress-related disorders (p1255–1256)
- **Acute stress reaction:** after a traumatic event, "daze", then withdrawal or agitation, often anxiety. Resolves within a few days.
- **Adjustment disorder:** a less severe but longer reaction to a major stressor (e.g. a serious diagnosis); depression and/or anxiety not meeting full criteria. Onset within a month. Pathological grief is a type. Management: support; most need no psychotropics.
- **PTSD:** a delayed and/or prolonged response to a life-threatening or catastrophic event (disasters, terrorism, serious accidents, witnessing violent death, sometimes ICU or distressing treatment).
  - Onset is a few days to several months after the event.
  - **Symptoms:** flashbacks; nightmares; autonomic arousal (palpitations, enhanced startle); emotional numbing; avoidance of reminders. Often with anxiety, depression, alcohol or drug misuse.
  - **Management:** early on, support, advice and the chance for emotional catharsis (routine debriefing may be harmful). Established PTSD: CBT, EMDR, stress management; antidepressants moderately effective.
  - **Prognosis:** most recover in 2–3 years; a small proportion become chronic.
